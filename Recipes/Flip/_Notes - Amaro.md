# The Seasoning: Amaro (pp258-, condensed)

Amaro isn't in the classic Flip, but it's worth exploring: the spice garnish on top of a Flip (nutmeg's aroma and flavor) is the role amaro can play, since it's packed with spices and bitterness. See [[Amaro]] (category note).

Amaro (plural amari) is Italian for "bitter". Most are made by macerating herbs, barks, roots, flowers, citrus and other botanicals in an alcohol base (usually neutral grain), then sweetened and sometimes aged. They defy categorization; the simplest definition is a bittersweet liqueur. There are no universal regulations. The authors group them by where they sit on the bitter and sweet spectrums: aperitivi, light amari, medium amari and dense amari. They can season, add complexity, accent a spirit or fortified wine, or be the core.

## Aperitivi
The lightest end: low-alcohol, slightly bitter, relatively dry drinks served before a meal to stimulate the appetite; stars of Highballs and spritzes with seltzer or prosecco.
- [[Aperol]]: 11% ABV, the lightest aperitivo; fresh oranges and rhubarb with a mild bitter undertone. Versatile: tiny amounts to boost the bitterness of grapefruit juice or liqueur, in a split base, or as the star. Used most in citrusy drinks like the [[Aperol Spritz]], as a seasoning for sours (High Five, p133), and in Martini variations.

## Light, medium and dense amari (pp259-261, condensed)
- **Aperitivi**: [[Aperol]], [[Campari]], [[Suze]].
- **Light amari** balance light sweetness with pleasant bitterness; usable in a split base in citrusy or boozy drinks, or in small amounts to accent a core spirit like a fruit or herbal liqueur: [[Amaro Meletti]], [[Amaro Montenegro]], [[Amaro Nonino]].
- **Medium amari** have more density, deep red or amber color, more sweetness and burnt caramel, and a more bitter finish. Used in smaller amounts as seasoning, e.g. in Martini or Manhattan riffs where amaro replaces some vermouth (skip the aromatic bitters): [[Amaro Averna]], [[Amaro CioCiaro]], [[Bigallet China-China Amer]], [[Cynar]], [[Ramazzotti]].
- **Dense amari** are sweeter, more bitter, or both, and noticeably distinctive; often a major component, or occasionally a tiny amount beneath the core flavor (Black Forest, p278): [[Fernet-Branca]], [[Luxardo Amaro Abano]].

## Ways to use amari in cocktails (p261)
- **Seasoning**: a dash to a teaspoon, like aromatic bitters; can bind ingredients that otherwise don't connect.
- **Vermouth substitute**: only sometimes works; a light amaro can take the place of sweet vermouth.
- **Replace part of the vermouth and the bitters**: in a Manhattan riff, drop the aromatic bitters and a bit of vermouth and add 1/4 to 1/2 oz amaro.
- **Split base**: any style, depending on bitterness tolerance; dense amari like Fernet may be too heavy-handed.
- **As the base**: needs a higher-proof bottle (ideally 30% ABV or more) and enough built-in balance; medium amari work best.
