# Sherry (pp244-246, condensed)

## History
Sherry was as common a base as other spirits in the late-19th-century cocktail boom (the Sherry Cobbler, p37, was a star). Its popularity faded after phylloxera devastated vineyards around 1900, then production lapsed through both world wars and Prohibition. Quality sherry has since returned and is a valuable cocktail ingredient.

## Styles
- **Fino**: dryness and salinity. **Amontillado**: aroma and a dry finish. **Oloroso**: density and a raisiny flavor. **Pedro Ximenez**: juicy sweetness. Sherry ranges from bone-dry to supersweet due to two aging methods.
- **Biological aging**: casks filled only four-fifths full let a layer of yeast (flor) grow on top, protecting the wine from oxidation while feeding on it. Produces the driest sherries, fino and manzanilla; also the first step for amontillado and palo cortado, which then age oxidatively.
- **Oxidative aging**: no flor, so oxygen interacts with the sherry, which develops nutty flavors and concentrates as vapors escape. Gives oloroso and Pedro Ximenez.
- **Solera**: fractional aging, blending multiple vintages through layers of barrels. Part of the oldest barrels (the solera) is bottled and replaced from the next-oldest, and so on up, with new wine (sobretabla) topping the top layer. Barrels are never emptied, so a solera can contain trace amounts of very old wine and is remarkably consistent year to year.

## How we use sherry in cocktails (p245)
1. Simple substitution for a similar ingredient (fino for dry vermouth, oloroso for sweet vermouth).
2. As the sole base, building on a style or bottle's traits.
3. As a split base, typically slightly more sherry than spirit (1 or 1 1/2 oz sherry plus 1 oz spirit), good for both citrusy and spirituous drinks. Book examples: Fair Game (p280), La Valencia (p188), Fuji Legend (p280).
4. In tiny, nearly imperceptible amounts as seasoning, especially salty finos and amontillados.

## Fino and manzanilla (p246)
Driest end of the spectrum: fino is first-press Palomino grapes fortified to about 15% ABV. Finos from the seaside town of Sanlucar de Barrameda are labeled manzanilla for their noticeably briny character. They're delicate, so use them as a base in low-ABV drinks like spritzes or Highballs, or as a complement to less assertive spirits like vodka or blanco tequila, often in 1 tsp to 1/2 oz amounts for salinity and creamy yeastiness (as in the Celebrate, p29). They're very fragile: once opened, cork, refrigerate and use within a week or so, and look for a recent bottling date (avoid more than a couple of years old).

**Recommended bottles:** [[Gonzalez Byass Tio Pepe Fino]] (benchmark, utility player); [[La Gitana manzanilla sherry]] (their go-to: dry, salty, mildly yeasty, bread-like; works as base or modifier); [[Lustau Jarana fino sherry]] (textbook fino, dry and yeasty with subtle almond); [[Lustau La Ina fino sherry]] (a bit more body).

**En rama** (raw) sherries are bottled with very little filtration: yeastier, silkier, but they deteriorate quickly even unopened, so get the freshest bottle possible. Quality en rama finos and manzanillas are rarely available outside Spain.

## Amontillado, palo cortado, oloroso and sweet sherries (pp247-249, condensed)
- **Amontillado**: both biological and oxidative aging, so dry and yeasty plus concentrated and nutty. Valuable and versatile in cocktails, with a strong affinity for aged spirits, especially bourbon and Cognac (nuttiness accents vanilla and baking spices). Bottles: [[Barbadillo Principe Amontillado]] (always salty, so it seasons and can brighten citrus or curb bitterness), [[Lustau Los Arcos amontillado sherry]] (rich nose, surprisingly dry).
- **Palo cortado**: smells like an oloroso and tastes like an amontillado (richer coffee and slight molasses aroma, raisiny, dry finish). The key qualities are lost in complicated drinks, so use it as the core. Bottle: [[Lustau Peninsula Palo Cortado]].
- **Oloroso**: richest dry sherry (fortified to 17% ABV to prevent flor, solera aged); sweet nose of figs and raisins with a dry backbone. Like sweet vermouth minus the spices, botanicals and bitters, so add those back when substituting; has the body to stand up to cream in decadent drinks like the Flip. Bottle: [[Lustau Almacenista Pata de Gallina Oloroso]].
- **Sweet sherries** (Pedro Ximenez and Moscatel): very concentrated dessert wines. Use PX like a liqueur or sweetener ("nature's finest simple syrup") but remember it adds proof. Moscatel is floral and perfumed, so use modest amounts like a floral liqueur. Bottles: [[Gonzalez Byass Noe Pedro Ximenez]], [[Lustau San Emilio Pedro Ximenez]], [[Lustau Emilin Moscatel]].
- **Cream sherry**: blends of styles (dry to sweet). Often associated with inferior cooking bottlings, but some are excellent and useful. Bottles: [[Gonzalez Byass Matusalem oloroso sherry]] (about 30 years, technically a cream sherry), [[Lustau East India solera sherry]] (a good sweet-vermouth stand-in), [[Williams & Humbert Dry Sack sherry]] (amontillado stand-in).
