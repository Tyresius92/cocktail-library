The defining traits of the Daiquiri and its extended family of cocktails ("Daiquiri Orthodoxy", p105):
- A Daiquiri is composed of a spirit, citrus and a sweetener, typically rum, lime juice and simple syrup.
- A Daiquiri is flexible in regard to the proportions of citrus to sweetener, which depend on the drinker's preference and the acidity and sweetness of the juice.
- A Daiquiri requires a level of improvisation due to the inconsistency of citrus juices.

Root recipes: [[Daiquiri]] (classic, p103), [[Ideal Daiquiri]] (the book's version, p104), [[Basic Sour]] (the formula, p106).

## The Drink of a Thousand Faces (p105, condensed)
Not the slushie kind from Bourbon Street or the oversized resort blender drink. The Daiquiri at the best cocktail bars is a foggy white, frothy tipple in a dainty stemmed glass, the "ultimate handshake" because making it well takes more attention to technique than most cocktails: freshness of lime juice, choice of rum, shaking method, glass temperature. It rewards understanding how strong, sweet and sour collaborate, and thinking on your feet (citrus varies in acidity and sweetness from fruit to fruit; adjust).

## History with a grain of salt (p106)
Legend credits Jennings Cox, an American mining engineer in Cuba, who in 1898 ran out of gin at a dinner party and made a punch of local rum, citrus, sugar and mineral water, naming it after a beach. The book takes such stories with a grain of salt and includes little history; it recommends David Wondrich for the real thing.
