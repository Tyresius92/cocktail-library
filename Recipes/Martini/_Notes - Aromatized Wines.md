# Aromatized Wines: Vermouth and Others

Condensed from pp. 72-74. The Martini's collaborator for gin or vodka is an aromatized wine, most commonly vermouth: usually a fairly neutral white wine flavored with herbs, barks or citrus and fortified (16-18% ABV after sugar is added). The extra proof preserves the wine and lets it stand up to gin or vodka. Residual sweetness is vital in balancing a Martini because it curbs the strength of a high-proof spirit.

- Vermouth has a shelf life like wine: buy the smallest bottle (375 ml) and use it fresh (for the balance experiment, a bottle open no more than a week).
- Herbs are macerated for weeks (commonly wormwood, chamomile, gentian; wormwood is often omitted or minimal because of early-1900s regulations). Sugar is added: a little for dry, more for blanc, lots for sweet.
- History: credit goes to Antonio Benedetto Carpano (1786) for modern vermouth: **sweet / red / Italian**, dark and often bitter, sweet cherry and vanilla (the color comes from flavorings and caramel, not the wine). The French adopted it with alpine ingredients and less sweetness: **dry / French**, clear and colorless. **Blanc (French) / bianco (Italian)**: sweetness like red but fewer spicy flavors and more herbs (thyme), clear; French blancs delicate, sweet and herbal, Italian biancos amplified with a big vanilla note.
- The book's favorites usually come from the style's country of origin: Italian brand for sweet, French for dry; for blanc/bianco it depends, most often Dolin blanc.
- Other aromatized wines usually emphasize a single flavoring or a few, can differ greatly in flavor or alcohol, and need more balance adjustment.
