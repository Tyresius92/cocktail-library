# The Core and Seasoning: Whisky (pp. 202-207, condensed)

All whiskies are made from grains cooked into a mash, distilled and aged in oak; differences come from the grains used, how they're processed, the distillation, the barrels, the aging time, and whether it's bottled from the cask or blended. The authors mostly use American whiskey (see the Old-Fashioned chapter) but use more international whiskies as affordable bottles turn up. Part of the [[_Notes]] for the Highball.

## Scotch whisky (p203-205)
Two broad categories: single malt and blended; a single malt isn't inherently better than a blend, just different. There is a wide gap between good-quality, affordable and mixable scotches.

### Single-malt scotch
Only barley and water; distilled twice in copper pot stills, aged at least three years in oak, from one distillery (may blend different ages; an age statement is the youngest whisky in the blend).
- **Speyside** (Highlands): the heart of scotch; light and floral (Glenlivet) or rich and fruity (Balvenie, from sherry casks). [[The Glenlivet 12-Year]]
- **Lowlands**: lighter-bodied, a bit sweeter, little peat smoke; a broad substrate to build flavor on. [[Auchentoshan Three Wood]]
- **Islay**: the most distinctive; intensely smoky and rich, and the smoke can overpower, so split it with a blended scotch (Smoke and Mirrors, p131), keep it in a dasher bottle for small pops of flavor or an atomizer to spray atop a cocktail for savory aroma (a trick from Sam Ross's Penicillin, p282). [[Bowmore 12-Year]], [[Laphroaig 10-year scotch]]
- **Orkney**: [[Highland Park 12-year scotch]]

**Using single malt (and other expensive booze) in cocktails (p204):** purists object even to an ice cube, but the authors feel cocktails show the ultimate respect when they emphasize the spirit's qualities. At home, to economize use a single malt to accent another whisky: base on an affordable blend (Famous Grouse) with a small amount of single malt, particularly effective for Islay. Or take inspiration from the geography and climate of the whisky: salty manzanilla sherry to echo the sea, floral St-Germain to amplify heather.

### Blended scotch (p205)
Blends dominated the category until single malts got expensive; innovators like John Glaser of Compass Box are restoring honor to blends. Affordable and versatile; the authors want an even balance between richness and aging spice. Favorites have appley fruitiness and a smoked tobacco flavor that works especially well in citrusy drinks and in stirred cocktails.
- [[Compass Box Asyla]], [[Famous Grouse Scotch]], [[Compass Box Oak Cross]]

## Irish whiskey (p205-206)
One of the first whiskeys, oscillating between prominence and near extinction, now experiencing a renaissance. Most is distilled three times, not two, in typically much larger pot stills; though there are single-malt equivalents, Irish whiskey favors blending. The authors don't focus on style or region (the industry is consolidated into a few distilleries). Lighter blends (Paddy, Jameson, Tullamore Dew) are soft and sweet like a young bourbon: tasty in citrusy cocktails but a little flat as the star of Manhattans or Old-Fashioneds. **Pure pot still** Irish whiskey has depth and body for many applications; the authors love pairing it with ingredients that emphasize its qualities.
- [[Bushmills Original Irish whiskey]], [[Redbreast 12-year Irish whiskey]]

## Japanese whisky (p206-)
From obscure to fanatical reputation, though scarce in the US. Few regulations; shaped by tradition. Many producers use the same barrels as elsewhere (bourbon, sherry, port) and also Japanese mizunara oak and plum wine casks, so the range goes from near-single-malt scotch to blends like Scotland's and Ireland's to styles unique to Japan. Too diverse for general guidelines; the recommended bottles are described by which type of whisky (scotch single malt or blend, Irish blend or pot still, American) they most resemble. 
- [[Hakushu 12-year Japanese whisky]]: resembles single-malt scotch; the benchmark for the book's Highball
- [[Nikka Coffey Grain Whisky]]: between Irish whiskey and bourbon (mostly corn, continuous Coffey still)
- [[Suntory Toki]]: smooth, light blend; affordable (~$45) gentle introduction
Most Japanese whisky is exported in limited quantities and tends to be expensive.

## Other world whiskeys (p207)
Dominated by America, Scotland, Ireland, Canada and Japan, but good aged whiskey now comes from India, Switzerland, Sweden, Denmark, Germany, Austria, France, South Africa, Australia, Thailand and New Zealand. If the production method resembles a familiar style, that predicts quality and usability in cocktails; also study where it comes from and the climate.

## Experimenting with the Core and Seasoning (p208)
- With blended scotch alone, use a split core to bring in flavors with an affinity for the whisky. Fortified wines work because many scotch barrels held sherry, madeira and port. American whiskey (especially bourbon) is another cohort, though its aggressive oak can overpower a blend.
- Two ways: mostly scotch (1 1/2 oz) with a little bourbon (1/2 oz) for a scotch-focused drink with added spice; or inverse proportions (bourbon-heavy with a bit of scotch) for American-whiskey spice with a soft, earthy, apple-like undertone.
- Other spirits also work in Highballs because seltzer suits any spirit and its bubbles enhance aroma and add an acidic note: blanco tequila turns citrusy; gin with seltzer (instead of tonic) lets botanicals blossom.
- Next step: substitute other booze, especially fortified wines and bitter aperitifs, as in the classic [[Americano]].
