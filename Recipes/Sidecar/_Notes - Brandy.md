# The Core: Brandy (pp. 156-162, condensed)

Cognac gives the Sidecar a broad foundation: rich enough to wrangle the acidity of lemon and structured enough to mingle with Cointreau's bright citrus, spiciness and mild sweetness. *Brandy* is a catchall term for spirits distilled from fruits, including grape brandies, apple brandies and eaux-de-vie. Grape brandy is by far the most common worldwide, with Cognac the most popular; there is also Armagnac, pisco (Peru and Chile), and good American brandies. Barrel aging gives rich, juicy brandies with vanilla and spice; unaged brandies are fiery and sharp, the unadulterated essence of the grapes. Different styles are rarely interchangeable: two Cognacs of similar age can swap, but aged vs unaged changes the balance. For the Sidecar a moderately aged Cognac supplies richness; a younger one falls flat.

## Cognac (p156-157)
Southwestern France; grape wine distilled in alembic stills and aged in oak. Blending is even more vital than distilling or aging: each house produces a consistent style year to year. Grades by age: **VS, VSOP, XO**; *hors d'age* is officially equal to XO but producers use it for bottles beyond XO. Cognac is pricey, so the authors mostly use VS and VSOP: VSOPs are full in flavor and body and versatile (often a cooked banana-like flavor); VS is woodsy and too young for spirituous cocktails but fine for citrusy ones like the Sidecar. See [[Cognac]].
- Bottles: [[Hennessy VS]], [[Paul Beau VS]], [[H by Hine VSOP]], [[Park VSOP]], [[Pierre Ferrand 1840 Cognac]] (45% ABV, mixes like a VSOP, holds its own with rye), [[Pierre Ferrand Ambre Cognac]] (the go-to)
- Splurge: [[A Ridiculously Good and Unnecessary Sidecar]] with [[Paul Beau Hors d'Age Cognac]]

## Armagnac (p157-158)
A close cousin to Cognac (southwest France, similar ingredients) but spicier: "bourbon is to rye as Cognac is to Armagnac". Often bottled in dated vintages that vary so much that the authors can't recommend vintages; blended Armagnacs that reach the US tend to be more affordable and use the same VS, VSOP, XO, hors d'age grading.
- Bottles: [[Tariquet Classique VS Bas-Armagnac]], [[Tariquet VSOP Bas-Armagnac]], [[Domaine d'Esperance Blanche Armagnac]] (unaged: savory, almonds and white pepper)

## Spanish brandy (p158)
Richer, darker, often a bit sweeter than French. Mostly Brandy de Jerez, rich and sweet, from the southern tip of Spain. Works like Cognac and Armagnac but with pronounced richness from the aging process; when working with heavily aged Spanish brandies, add just a small amount to dose the cocktail with a spiced fruitcake kick (more overwhelms). The exception is an Old-Fashioned, a great template for letting a very old Spanish brandy shine.
- Bottles: [[Cardenal Mendoza Solera Gran Reserva]], [[Gran Duque de Alba Solera Gran Reserva]], [[Lustau Brandy de Jerez Solera]]

## Pisco (p159)
Pisco and its cousin singani are South American brandies from intensely floral grapes, often bottled unaged, so they work very differently from European aged brandies. Almost inseparable from the [[Pisco Sour]]; the sharp, floral spirit is a flavorful, aromatic base for shaken citrusy cocktails. Particularly good with amontillado-style sherry (Daisy Gun, p279), and increasingly versatile in stirred cocktails. Used much like blanco tequila, but where tequila gives vegetal character, pisco gives floral aroma with an intense bite, which can be curbed with blanc vermouth or another semisweet fortified wine.
- Bottles: [[Campo de Encanto Grand and Noble pisco]], [[Capurro Pisco Acholado]], [[Macchu Pisco]]

## Singani (p159-160)
Bolivia's native unaged grape brandy, grown on a tiny arid region in southern Bolivia at 5,200 feet; highly aromatic Muscat de Alexandria grapes. Use as with pisco but expect more floral character; bolster a fortified wine (Class Act, p279) or collaborate with another base spirit ([[Cat Video]]). In Bolivia it's a *chuflay*: singani, ginger ale and a slice of lime. Bottle: [[Singani 63]].

## Apple brandy (p160-161)
Smooth and rich or spicy and pungent depending on distillation and aging, but always an inherent impression of apples. Two main types:
- **Calvados** (France): from one or many of the 200 varieties of apples and pears allowed by law, fermented into cider, distilled and aged in oak at least two years. Classified by region (Pays d'Auge is the most restricted) and age. The authors favor Calvados aged at least three years; younger lacks depth and gets lost, older is too pricey. Bottles: [[Busnel Pays d'Auge VSOP Calvados]], [[Dupont Pays d'Auge Reserve]].
- **American apple brandy**: as old as America; the first commercial US distillery (1780) made it. Laird's is the benchmark and the American style is more akin to whiskey (aged in charred oak) than to Calvados, which is a close cousin of Cognac. Applejack is legally a blend of apple brandy and neutral grain spirits aged at least four years in used bourbon barrels; the authors prefer 100-proof Laird's straight apple brandy, made only from apples. Whiskey-style brandies work like bourbon in cocktails; French-style ones work like Calvados. Since "applejack" used to mean pure apple brandy, use one of the recommended bottles for old-school recipes.
- Bottles: [[Black Dirt Apple Jack]], [[Clear Creek 2-Year Apple Brandy]], [[Clear Creek 8-Year Apple Brandy]], [[Copper & Kings Floodwall Apple Brandy]], [[Laird's 100-proof straight apple brandy]], [[Laird's 88-proof 12-Year Apple Brandy]]

## Eau-de-vie, or fruit brandy (p162)
"Water of life": clear brandies made with fruits other than grapes, mostly unaged, so flavor and aroma depend on refined distilling. Two strategies: 1/2 to 3/4 oz as part of a split base, or 1 teaspoon to 1/4 oz in a low-ABV cocktail that lacks body to add a flavorful punch.
- **Pear (Poire Williams)**: acts almost like a concentrated flavor extract; in a tiny proportion with an apple brandy base it lifts and amplifies the apple ("Technicolor apple"); also good with herbal flavors (Jet Set, p281). Bottles: [[Clear Creek pear brandy]], [[Massenez Poire Williams]].
- **Cherry (kirsch / kirschwasser)**: all alcoholic fire and grit on the nose, but after a few sips you smell cherry. Use in small quantities, generally 1 teaspoon to 1/4 oz (Rye Pie, p283). Bottles: [[Clear Creek Cherry Brandy]], [[Massenez Kirsch Vieux]].
- Apricot: a floral, dry eau-de-vie; a little goes a long way (Rendezvous in Chennai, p283; Unidentified Floral Objects, p284). [[Blume Marillen Apricot Eau-de-Vie]].
