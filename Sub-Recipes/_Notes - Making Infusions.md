# Technique: Making Infusions

Condensed from pp. 92-99. Infusions add flavor to a base spirit or modifier and can then be swapped into recipes. The book usually starts from a desired final flavor profile, picks a base formula from one of the six root recipes, and builds from flavor pairings (e.g. lemon + honey + chamomile with a warming spirit like Calvados: a sour, a Collins, or a toddy such as [[Gun Club Toddy]]). *The Flavor Bible* is recommended for pairings.

- Alcohol is a great solvent but can pull out unwanted flavors (raspberries soaked too long turn bitter from the seeds). Fresh fruit can lose aromatics by macerating too long or by high heat, so choose the method per ingredient: cold, room-temperature, sous vide, pressurized, or centrifuge.
- Base liquid: spirits at 40-50% ABV work best for any technique; higher proof (e.g. Everclear) extracts fast but pulls bitterness (good for bitters, not fresh ingredients); for bitter fresh ingredients like citrus peel use the low end. Lower-ABV bases (liqueurs, fortified wines, still wine) work but need protection from oxidation.
- Proof also sets timing: higher-proof spirits infuse faster. Examples: Cardamom-Infused St-Germain (20% ABV) about 12 hours; Madras Curry-Infused Gin (44% ABV) about 15 minutes.

## Cold infusions (p. 94-95)
Chilling slows extraction: mellow, deep, complex (coffee beans), but only for ingredients without unpleasant flavors that surface over time. Beware pungent ingredients in the freezer or fridge.
- **Fat washing** (Don Lee and Eben Freeman): combine alcohol with a fat (butter, cream, oil, animal fat) to add richness, then freeze so the fat solidifies. Melt solid fat gently, combine with the spirit in a wide container, stir, cover and freeze until the fat forms a layer (usually 12 hours or less). Poke a hole, drain the liquid, strain through a cheesecloth-lined sieve or a Superbag. Refrigerate (better flavor retention than room temperature). Examples: Milk-Washed Rum (p291), Coconut-Infused Bourbon (p289).
- **Eggs**: line a shallow resealable container with paper towels, layer the flavoring ingredient (e.g. lavender), arrange whole uncooked eggs in their shells in a single layer on top, seal and refrigerate overnight.

## Room-temperature infusions (p. 95)
For ingredients that infuse in an hour or less and are temperamental (jalapeno in tequila overinfuses in a heartbeat; black tea turns tannic). Weigh, combine, and taste every minute or so at first, then every 15 minutes, then hourly; keep covered when not tasting. Strain through a fine-mesh sieve lined with cheesecloth or a Superbag, then refrigerate. Examples: Thai Chile-Infused Bourbon (about 5 minutes or less!), Oolong-Infused Vodka (about 20 minutes, a color cue). Chiles vary, so taste every time.

## Sous vide infusions (p. 96)
Heat speeds infusion and holding a precise temperature without evaporation gives subtler, more nuanced results (e.g. Coconut-Infused Bourbon tastes of fresh coconut). Typically 135-145F: lower for delicate fruit, higher for sturdy coconut, nuts or dried spices. Method: water basin plus circulator at the set temperature; weigh ingredients into a bowl, transfer to a heatproof resealable bag and remove the air by dipping the bag in the water (leave the seal open until submerged), cook for the specified time, move the bag to an ice bath (condenses vapor and preserves the alcohol), strain through a fine-mesh sieve, refrigerate.

## Pressurized infusions (p. 97-98)
For fragile ingredients that perish quickly or change with heat.
- **Rapid (iSi whipper + N2O)**: compressed gas forces liquid into the solid's cells and the release pulls it back out carrying flavor. Great for fresh herbs and cacao nibs. About 10 minutes, no off-flavors. Fill the whipper (1 quart, preferably 1-liter Thermo Whip) no higher than Max, charge with an N2O cartridge, shake about five times, charge again with a second cartridge and shake; let sit under pressure for 10 minutes, shaking about every 30 seconds. Vent at a 45-degree angle into a container as fast as possible (the quicker the venting, the better the infusion) until no bubbling is audible. Strain through a fine-mesh sieve or Superbag, funnel back into the bottle, refrigerate. Best within a month (delicate herbs and citrus zest, within a week).
- **Vacuum (chamber vacuum machine)**: removes air so the liquid is forced into the solid's pores, then pulled back out when pressure returns. Put the ingredients in a wide, high-walled (2+ inch) container, cover with plastic wrap with about ten holes poked in it, run at full vacuum for 1 minute (dramatic boiling; stop if it risks boiling over); repeat at least twice. Taste with a straw. For more flavor: more cycles, or run full vacuum and turn the machine off so it holds for about 10 minutes. Strain, refrigerate; best within 1 week (usable up to 4 weeks).

## Centrifuge infusions (p. 99)
Learned from Dave Arnold; requires a centrifuge. Blend the solids with booze, strain, weigh the liquid, stir in 0.2% of its weight in Pectinex Ultra SP-L (rest 15 minutes), divide among centrifuge containers weighed to exactly equal weights (balance matters; off-balance centrifuges are dangerous), spin at 4,500 rpm for 12 minutes, carefully strain through a paper coffee filter or Superbag without disturbing the solids, strain again if needed, and refrigerate. Examples: Graham Cracker-Infused Bourbon (p290, for the Campfire), Strawberry-Infused Cognac and Mezcal (p293).
- Some liquid from the flavoring ends up in the infusion (can shorten shelf life and lower proof).
- Ratios by weight (flavoring : booze): dry ingredients such as dried fruit or graham crackers 1:4; water-containing ingredients like bananas or strawberries 1:2; very subtle flavors like watermelon 1:1.
